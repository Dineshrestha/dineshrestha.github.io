Automated PLSS & Road Attribution

A reusable ArcGIS Pro workflow that replaces manual lookups with consistent spatial rules—assigning township, range, section, state, county, nearby structures, road ownership, transmission-line relationships, and calculated road length to infrastructure features.

THE STORY

The roads were mapped. The business-ready attributes were not.

A transmission-line team needed access-road records enriched with PLSS location, state, county, ownership, nearby structures, transmission-line relationships, and reliable road length. Looking up those values one road at a time was slow—and roads crossing multiple boundaries made “the first intersect” an unreliable answer.

We converted reviewer judgment into spatial rules. The roadmap prioritized authoritative references, used longest-overlap logic for ambiguous polygons, added fallback coverage where primary PLSS data were incomplete, normalized identifiers, and retained one defensible record per road.

Boundary cases forced the design beyond a basic spatial join. Source-specific ownership fields, missing coverage, nearest-structure logic, and many-to-many intersections were handled explicitly. The final batch workflow produced standardized, reviewable attribution in minutes rather than hours.

Manual attribution was slow—and spatially ambiguous.

Infrastructure access-road records needed consistent location and relationship attributes before they could move into downstream tracking and reporting. A feature could cross several PLSS polygons, counties, parcels, or ownership areas, and simple point-in-polygon logic could return the wrong answer. Missing coverage and inconsistent identifier formats added another layer of risk.

01 / COVERAGE

Multiple reference sources

Arizona and New Mexico required different authoritative reference layers, including a New Mexico fallback where the primary township/range coverage was incomplete.

02 / OVERLAP

More than one possible match

Roads often crossed multiple administrative or PLSS polygons, so attribution needed a defensible dominant-overlap rule rather than an arbitrary first match.

03 / GOVERNANCE

Downstream consistency mattered

Identifiers, dates, ownership categories, classifications, and calculated lengths had to match established schemas and reporting conventions.

A spatial decision engine instead of repeated lookups.

KEY DESIGN CHOICE

Longest overlap—not the first intersect

When a road crossed more than one county, PLSS section, or ownership polygon, the workflow segmented the overlap and retained the polygon containing the greatest road length. This reduced duplicate records and made the selected value explainable during QA.

The same principle was used to preserve one final record per access road rather than allowing many-to-many spatial joins to inflate the output.

Rules that handle real-world exceptions.

The most valuable part of the automation was not running tools faster—it was converting reviewer decisions and project conventions into repeatable logic.

New Mexico PLSS fallback

Use a secondary BLM PLSS source when the primary township/range layer does not cover the feature.

Identifier normalization

Standardize section formatting and normalize township/range values to the required final format.

Road ownership mapping

Translate source-specific Arizona and New Mexico ownership fields into a consistent reporting category, including private ownership.

Transmission-line classification

Classify each feature as “Crosses T-Line” or “Abutting or Connecting” using the feature’s spatial relationship.

Nearest-structure assignment

Use a near-table workflow to identify the closest relevant structure and carry its identifier into the result.

Protected source resolution

Cache service data into a temporary geodatabase before analysis so the workflow remains stable and preserves original sources.

PERFORMANCE

Hours of manual work became a repeatable batch

A representative combined attribution run processed roughly 40 features in about 30–40 minutes, replacing several hours of manual research and editing. After refinement, one corrected PLSS batch completed in approximately 11 minutes.

The output preserved the original data, wrote results to a controlled geodatabase, and could be added back to the ArcGIS Pro map for immediate review.
