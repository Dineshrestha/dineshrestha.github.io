THE STORY

One viewshed took three hours. The next request included about 90 sites.

A transmission project needed to identify structures potentially visible from cultural-resource sites. The first single-site analysis produced a complete answer, but repeating it manually across approximately 90 point, line, and polygon resources would require at least 270 labor hours.

We redesigned the method for scale without reducing each site to a convenient point. Lines and polygons received multiple observer locations because a midpoint or centroid could miss visibility elsewhere on the resource. Terrain coverage, observer spacing, raster size, and reporting were then tested as one connected system.

The original 10-meter terrain and dense sampling plan was computationally expensive. Testing led to a project-wide 30-meter DEM, configurable 500-meter spacing, and optional raster retention. The result was a transferable batch toolbox and an estimated 94% reduction in labor.

CRITICAL DESIGN DECISION

Represent the entire site—not one convenient location

A line midpoint or polygon centroid represents only one viewpoint. A tower hidden from that location may still be visible from another part of the same cultural resource. Multiple observers were therefore distributed across extended geometry so “potentially visible from the site” reflected the site as a whole.

After testing denser alternatives, configurable 500-meter spacing balanced spatial representation with production runtime.

TERRAIN STRATEGY

Resolution was selected through performance testing

Ten-meter elevation tiles were downloaded, clipped to a corridor buffered beyond the five-mile search distance, and mosaicked into a continuous surface. Testing showed the cell count was too costly for a many-observer, many-site production run.

A project-wide 30-meter DEM dramatically reduced the processing burden while preserving a practical terrain surface for batch screening.

Automation controls the expensive parts.

The solution exposes the decisions that matter so another GIS analyst can rerun the study when sites, structures, terrain, or reporting needs change.

Consolidated reporting
